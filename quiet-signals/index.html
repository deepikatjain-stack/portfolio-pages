Quiet Signals

You can't see the control on an earbud while you're wearing it. I designed how a pair of earbuds should tell you what your finger just did, using sound, touch and light.

The problem

Earbud controls are used blind, and most of them answer badly.

You pause a song on the train, take a call while you're cooking, turn on noise blocking as a bus pulls up. You do all of it by touch. The only answer most earbuds give is a chime, and it often sounds the same no matter what happened.

I reviewed the control schemes that are common across wireless earbuds today against basic feedback heuristics (is there a response, is it specific, does it survive the setting you're in). Four failures kept coming up.

1

The press lands and nothing answers

Single, double and triple presses wait to see if another press is coming. That wait has no feedback, so people press again and skip a track they meant to pause.

2

One chime covers several meanings

Noise blocking on and noise blocking off often share a tone. You hear that something changed, but you still don't know which mode you're in.

3

Sound is the only channel

On a loud street, with the cue volume turned down, or for someone who is hard of hearing, an audio-only cue disappears.

4

Holds commit with no warning

Press and hold triggers at a fixed time you can't feel coming. Let go a little late and you've woken the voice assistant in a meeting.

Why I took this on

I've watched people use controls they can't look at.

At Volvo Group, I rode along with commercial truck drivers during live driving and watched how they used climate and secondary controls. Their eyes stayed on the road. The controls they trusted answered with something they could feel or hear, like a detent, a click or a tone, and the ones they avoided didn't.

An earbud stem has the same job with less room. There's no screen, very little surface, and you're usually doing something else at the time. In an earlier project, Humanoid Handoff, I designed a five-state light language for factory robots. Quiet Signals uses the same method, spread across three senses.

If you can't see the control, it has to answer through every other sense you have.

The language

Four rules, applied to every signal.

Before designing any one tone or buzz, I wrote the rules all of them would follow. I used the rules to settle arguments with myself later, when a cue felt fun but broke one of them.

Answer on contact

The moment a finger lands on the stem, the earbud gives a tiny tick and a short tap. You know you were heard before the earbud knows what you meant.

Shape follows meaning

Rising pitch means opening up: play, volume up, letting the room in. Falling pitch means closing down. Haptics and light follow the same direction.

At least two senses

Every signal reaches you through at least two of sound, touch and sight, so it still works when one of them is unavailable.

The common action gets the quietest cue

Play and pause happen all day, so their cue is the smallest. Rare changes that matter more, like a listening mode switch or hitting the volume limit, get longer and more distinct cues.

Rising: opening

Play, next track, volume up, Blend mode on, connected.

Falling: closing

Pause, previous track, volume down, noise blocking on, disconnected.

Flat: neutral

Listening mode off, a held press you let go of early, a setting returned to default.

The companion app, and why it's small

  • The app mirrors the earbuds. Change the mode on the stem and the segmented control moves with it. Change it in the app and the earbuds play the same cue they would for a stem press.
  • Signal settings live in a sheet you drag up. People can choose what a hold does, and whether signals are tones, spoken words or touch only. These settings change the prototype above, so try Spoken words or Touch only.
  • The sheet follows your finger 1:1 from where you grabbed it. When you let go, it uses your flick speed to decide whether to open or close, and it resists past its limits. You can grab it again while it's still moving.

Design decisions

Three calls I had to make, and what each one cost.

Multi-press timing

Answer the contact right away, and the meaning 300 ms later.

Supporting one, two and three presses means the earbud has to wait before it can act. I couldn't remove that wait, so I made it obvious. Each press gets an immediate 12 ms tick and an 8 ms tap. The action, with its own cue, comes after the 300 ms window closes.

Cost: pause feels 300 ms slower than a single-press-only scheme. I'd test whether people notice, and whether the immediate tick stops the double presses that skip tracks.

Hold to commit

Make the hold something you can feel filling up.

A hold starts after 250 ms of steady contact. From there, a rising hum and a quickening haptic build for 600 ms, and the ring on the prototype fills. If you let go early, nothing happens and you get a soft, flat cancel tone, so you can back out halfway through. If you keep holding, it commits.

Cost: it takes 850 ms in total, slower than the instant holds people may be used to. In exchange, an accidental grab while you adjust the fit no longer changes anything.

Volume limits

Push back at the edges.

Each volume step gets a tick whose pitch matches the new level. At maximum or minimum, the tick turns into a low, short thud with a stronger haptic, and the level bar stretches a little and springs back. That tells you there's nothing further without throwing an error at you.

Cost: one more sound to learn. I gave it the lowest pitch in the set so it can't be mistaken for a step.

The case light

One light, five states.

When the earbuds are in the case, the case light becomes the third channel. I kept the color set to amber, green, white and red, and used rhythm to separate states, so the light still reads for people with color vision differences. Tap a state to play it.

Motion values

The on-screen pieces use springs instead of fixed-duration animation, so they can be grabbed and redirected mid-motion. The sheet uses a damping ratio of 1.0 (no overshoot) and a 0.35 s response. When it's flicked, the damping drops to 0.82 so it settles with a small, earned bounce. Release velocity carries into the spring, and the snap target comes from where the flick was heading. With Reduce Motion on, the sheet and volume bar move without springing and the hero ripples stop.

Accessibility

Designed for the full range of hearing, sight and touch.

Two senses minimum

No event depends on one channel. Touch only mode keeps every haptic pattern and drops the tones, which works for Deaf and hard-of-hearing users and in quiet rooms.

Spoken words as an option

Spoken mode replaces tones with short phrases like "Noise blocking." It helps people who are still learning the tones, and anyone with a cognitive load they'd rather not add to.

Screen reader parity

The prototype announces every result through a live region, and every stem gesture has a keyboard and button equivalent. On the earbud, the same text goes to VoiceOver.

Text, contrast and motion

Type is set in rem and scales with the user's text size. The page follows light and dark appearance, Increase Contrast, Reduce Transparency and Reduce Motion.

How I'd test it

What I want to learn next.

So far this concept rests on desk research, a heuristic review and my own work on in-vehicle controls. I haven't run a user study on it yet. This is the study I'd run first.

  1. LearnabilityEight participants, no explanation of the tones. After five minutes of free use, can they say which mode they're in from the cue alone? Goal: 7 of 8.
  2. NoiseRepeat the mode-change task with recorded street noise playing. Compare Tones against Touch only to see how much meaning the haptics carry on their own.
  3. The 300 ms waitA/B test the immediate contact tick against no tick. Measure accidental double presses and ask which version felt faster.
  4. Hold to commitCount accidental holds while participants take the earbuds out and put them back in. Compare against a fixed one-second hold with no build-up.
  5. Inclusive sessionsRecruit at least two participants who are hard of hearing and one screen reader user. Their sessions decide whether Touch only and Spoken words ship as defaults or as options.

Reflection

What this project changed for me

I started by designing tones and quickly found I was making sound effects. Once I wrote the four rules, the tones stopped being arbitrary. They became a vocabulary, and the haptics and the light could follow the same grammar.

Building the prototype in code mattered too. A spring value or a 300 ms window reads fine in a static frame, and the only way to judge it was to tap it. If I took this further, I'd tune the haptics on real actuators with an engineer, because the vibration on a phone is only a rough stand-in for a motor the size of a grain of rice.
